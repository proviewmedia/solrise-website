How to read a METAR

A METAR (Meteorological Aerodrome Report) is a routine weather observation from an airport station, written in compact code. You read it left to right: station, day and time in UTC, wind, visibility, sky condition, temperature and dew point, then the altimeter setting.

What a METAR is

A METAR is a surface observation from an airport weather station: what that station actually measured, not a forecast. It is refreshed roughly once an hour, and more often if conditions change quickly. It is the same real-world data behind everyday weather numbers, just in the compact code aviation has used for decades.

Here is a sample report, decoded below:

KVGT 091853Z 12005KT 10SM FEW150 36/06 A3010

Decoding a METAR, piece by piece

CodeWhat it means
KVGTStation identifier: the airport's ICAO code.
091853ZDay of month and time, in UTC ("Zulu"): the 9th at 18:53 UTC.
12005KTWind: direction in degrees, then speed in knots. Here, from 120 degrees at 5 knots. "VRB" means variable direction.
10SMVisibility in statute miles. 10SM is usually the most the station reports.
CLR, FEW, SCT, BKN, OVCSky condition: clear, then increasing cloud cover (few, scattered, broken, overcast), with the cloud base in hundreds of feet. FEW150 is a few clouds at 15,000 ft.
36/06Temperature / dew point, in Celsius. An "M" in front means minus.
A3010Altimeter setting: local barometric pressure in inches of mercury (30.10 inHg here).
RMK ...Remarks: extra automated-station detail like pressure trends and precise temperatures. Usually skippable.

Source: FAA and National Weather Service METAR format.

Sky cover in eighths

CodeSky coveredWhat it looks like
CLR / SKC0 eighthsClear sky.
FEW1 to 2 eighthsA few clouds, up to a quarter of the sky.
SCT3 to 4 eighthsPartly cloudy, up to half the sky.
BKN5 to 7 eighthsMostly cloudy, with only gaps of blue.
OVC8 eighthsThe whole sky is covered.

Source: METAR sky cover categories (oktas).

In aviation, "few" or "scattered" clouds never count as a ceiling. Only a broken or overcast layer does, and the lowest one sets the ceiling.

Why a METAR changes

Each report is a fresh snapshot, so it changes whenever the weather at that station does. The routine report comes out about every hour. When something big changes in between, like a sudden drop in visibility, the station sends an extra report.

What a METAR means for you

Everyday

A METAR is a nearby, frequently updated snapshot of exactly what is happening outside right now. In the example above, 36/06 means 36°C (about 97°F) with a 6°C dew point: hot and dry.

Outdoors

The station may be several miles away and at a different elevation than your trailhead, so treat it as a nearby reference, not the exact conditions where you will be. Check the observation time: it is in UTC.

Flying

METAR wind directions are referenced to true north, while tower and ATIS winds are magnetic, so the numbers can differ. Check the age of the report before you launch; conditions can change well within an hour.

Did you know?

When weather changes significantly between routine hourly reports, stations send out an unscheduled special report called a SPECI.

Source: FAA

Common questions

What does Z mean in a METAR?

Z stands for Zulu, which means UTC. In 091853Z, the report is from the 9th of the month at 18:53 UTC.

How often is a METAR issued?

Roughly once an hour. When weather changes significantly between routine reports, the station sends an unscheduled special report called a SPECI.

What does M mean in front of a METAR temperature?

M means minus. M05/M10 is a temperature of -5°C with a dew point of -10°C.
